💗 Mingle — Designing Dating for Real Human Interaction

A dating app concept designed around a simple problem: meeting someone is easy — knowing what to say afterwards isn't. We designed Mingle to help people discover, approach and talk to new people without letting AI take over the interaction.

Team project with Hadi Jaber, Mohamad Hassan Ibrahim & Malek Turki.

The problem

Getting a match isn't the hardest part.

We started Mingle by looking at something that existing dating apps often leave unresolved: what happens after two people match?

For shy or introverted users, a blank chat can be surprisingly intimidating. At the same time, existing platforms can feel superficial, overwhelming, or disconnected from real-life interactions.

Our goal was therefore not simply to design another dating app. We wanted to explore how an interface could reduce the friction of starting a human connection.

Understanding the users

We started with people, not screens.

We surveyed 50 young adults and tested several existing dating apps ourselves, including Tinder, Hinge, Pure and Muzz.

The research highlighted recurring frustrations: difficulty starting conversations, superficial interactions, safety concerns, ghosting and interfaces that sometimes created unnecessary cognitive load.

What we found How it influenced Mingle
Starting conversations feels awkward Optional AI conversation starters
Dating apps can feel superficial Shared interests & compatibility
Long onboarding creates fatigue Progressive, multi-step signup
Conversations often die quickly Contextual conversation support

Personas & scenarios

Designing around moments of hesitation.

From the research, we created personas and scenarios to turn abstract findings into concrete situations.

One of them was Alex, an introverted student who matches with people but overthinks his first message. Another was Maria, a busy professional frustrated by shallow conversations that quickly disappear.

Design process

Paper first. Pixels later.

Before opening Figma, we sketched the main flows on paper: onboarding, discovery, matching, profiles, chat and AI assistance.

This let us experiment quickly without becoming attached to visual details. Once the information architecture felt clear, we translated those ideas into low-fidelity wireframes and progressively refined the interface.

Interaction design

Making the interface feel familiar — without making it mindless.

We deliberately reused familiar mobile interaction patterns so users would not need to learn a completely new interface. The bottom navigation keeps important actions within thumb reach, while progressive disclosure reduces the amount of information presented during onboarding.

Principle How we applied it
Learnability Familiar navigation and interaction patterns
Fitts' Law Important actions positioned within thumb reach
Progressive Disclosure Signup divided into small manageable steps
User Control AI assistance remains optional and editable

Designing the AI

The AI should help the conversation — then get out of the way.

The AI assistant was deliberately designed as a support tool rather than an automatic conversational agent. Suggestions could always be ignored, edited or dismissed.

We imagined the chat as three states: Discovery when a conversation is starting, Flow when people are talking naturally, and Stall when the conversation begins to fade.

In Flow mode, the interface suppresses unnecessary suggestions. In other words, when two people are already connecting, Mingle stops trying to help.

Prototype

From a Figma concept to an interactive application.

We eventually implemented a functional prototype covering the main experience: profile creation, a social feed, nearby discovery, matching, messaging and AI-assisted conversations.

The geo-social map was designed to make discovery feel connected to the user's real environment rather than being an endless stack of profiles.

User testing

Then we gave it back to users.

We evaluated the prototype using task-based usability testing and the think-aloud method. Participants were asked to create a profile, discover someone nearby and start a conversation using the AI assistance.

Rather than only measuring whether users completed each task, we listened to their hesitations and reactions — especially when interacting with the AI.

Testing suggested that the assistant could make conversation initiation feel easier, but it also exposed an important tension: too much assistance can reduce authenticity or make users feel influenced by the system.
